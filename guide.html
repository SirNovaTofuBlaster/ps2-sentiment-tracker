1. What the dashboard shows

A robot on GitHub reads gaming news sites, Reddit, forums, 4chan's game boards, YouTube channels and podcasts all day long, each on its own clock: 4chan every 15 minutes, news sites and forums every 30, Reddit and YouTube every hour, podcasts every 6 hours. For every headline, thread, video and episode it checks whether a PlayStation 2 game is mentioned, whether it sounds like remaster or remake news, and how positive the wording is. Every game it recognises gets links out to shops and price guides, and eBay's asking prices beside its name. The dashboard shows the result:

  • Eight pages, chosen with the tabs under the banner. Tracker has the numbers, the games named in the last 24 hours, the table and the sources. Demand is the Demand Index on a page of its own (see The Demand Index). Rarest lists the hundred rarest PAL games, the hundred rarest US games and the rarest official PS2 consoles, with their eBay prices (see The rarest games). Most mentioned ranks every PS2 game the feed names. eBay prices opens with the PS2 Price Index and then lists what used copies of every tracked game are listed for. Library shows every game as its box (see The Library). Gacha is a free capsule-machine game (see The Gacha). Underrated shows one game a day that lists of underrated PS2 games keep naming (see Underrated). On a phone the eight tabs sit in one row that you slide sideways with your finger (the open tab scrolls into view). On a screen wider than a phone, the number on a tab is how many games that page holds. Your browser's Back button returns to the page you were on, The My collection button, at the top right of the banner next to REFRESH, opens an eighth page that keeps the games you own and adds up what they are worth (see My collection). Adding #rare, #demand, #mentions, #prices, #library, #gacha, #underrated or #collection to the address opens a page directly.
  • Search and game pages: the search box above the tabs knows every game in the PS2 library, not only the ones in the news. Start typing (godh brings up God Hand; spaces and capitals do not matter) and pick a name with a click, or with the arrow keys and Enter. You land on that game's own page, which opens with its box picture, its name and a big Add to My collection button (press it and the same bar as in the Library slides up from the bottom: choose region and condition, optionally what you paid, then add; if you have no account yet it asks you to make one first). Below that are its eBay prices on every site, one card per site (the full detail the lists leave out), and the latest items from the feeds that name it (news, Reddit, forums, 4chan, YouTube, podcasts), newest first. A game nobody has named in the last two weeks gets an empty page that says so; it fills in by itself later. The address of a game page (#game=God%20Hand) can be bookmarked. Every game name in the lists sits in a blue box and is a link to the same page (in the last-24-hours list, the small i beside the name; the name itself still filters the table). Clicking the Spindle logo, or Back to the Tracker, returns to the home page and clears the search.
  • Named in the last 24 hours, first on the Tracker: the PS2 games named by anything published in the last day, and nothing else. Most of what the robot reads names no PS2 game at all, so this list is where to look first. It is ordered by price: first the games with an eBay UK median, the most expensive at the top down to the cheapest; then the games with only a US price, the same way (dollars and pounds are never compared); then the games with no price at all. Three buttons beside the heading flick between views. Highlights (the one it opens on) shows twelve games as cards, mixed on purpose: games named in the news, rare and uncommon ones, everyday favourites such as GTA San Andreas, underrated picks and the biggest price movers. It is the same all day and different tomorrow, and each card says why it is there. The other two change the order of the list: Most expensive and Most rare, which puts first the games with the fewest copies for sale on eBay (using eBay's own count where it is higher than ours): 10 or fewer, then up to 30, then up to 100, then the rest, and inside each group the dearer first. A game whose Wikipedia article calls it a collectible leads its group and wears a collectible tag. A game not yet checked on eBay comes last. A game with 10 copies or fewer wears a red scarce tag with the count, in either order: a rare game that suddenly gets named is the kind a collector may want to buy before the price moves. A game is checked on eBay soon after the feed first names it, so a brand-new oddity shows its count within the hour. Games at the same price, and the games without one, keep the usual order: the most headlines first, so one found only in the text under a headline comes later, then the most mentions, then the most recent. Each line says how many items named the game and which sources, with its prices. Click a game's name to jump to the table showing exactly the items that name it, from the whole two weeks and not only the last day; Back to every game above the table, or any change to the search box or the menus, returns the table to normal. The line under the heading says how many items were published in the last day and how many of them named a game. "Published" is the date the source gave the item, so when a source is first added, its older items go straight to the table. (A source that gives no dates has its items dated when the robot first sees them.) The list is as good as the name matching: a series still going today is counted for its PS2 game, and an ordinary phrase is sometimes taken for a title.
  • eBay median: beside a game's name, wherever one appears (the last 24 hours, Demand Index, Most Mentioned Games, the eBay prices tab and the table), you see the middle asking price of used copies on eBay US and eBay UK, and the cheapest of the four euro sites (Germany, France, Italy, Spain), marked from DE and so on. Click the game's name for everything else: every site, how many copies, the cheapest copy, the change since a week before, the price chart and links to eBay itself. Once a game has been priced for three days, a bracket beside the median on its page says how it moved since a week before (for the first week, since its oldest check that is at least three days old, and the hover says "3 days before" and so on): £73.49 (£2.30 ▲ 3.2%) means up £2.30, or 3.2%, and ▼ means down; (no change) means the same. Hover over the bracket for the week-old figure and its date. When a game's search words have just been changed, its bracket compares two different searches for a week, so a big jump then says more about the search than about the market. A game with no such line is one that isn't priced: see eBay asking prices.
  • The shelf, above the tabs on the Tracker page: up to 24 of the games named in the last 24 hours, rarest first (the fewest copies for sale on eBay, so everyday games such as God of War and Resident Evil 4 sit at the far end; games not checked on eBay yet come last), as box art (the number in the corner is how many items named it; under the name are three eBay median asking prices: US in dollars, UK in pounds and EU in euros, with a dash where nothing is priced, or n/a where the game never came out in that region, such as a US price for a PAL-only game (hover to see why); the euro figure is the cheapest euro site's, or, when none has one, the UK (else US) price converted at the European Central Bank rate and marked ≈; the EUR button converts the others too). Use the arrows at the left and right ends (or swipe) to move along it, and open a box to see that game's page. It holds the same games as "Named in the last 24 hours" below, in the order of its "Most rare" button, and is hidden on the other pages and when no game was named; a game without a cover shows its initials. Cover pictures come from the Libretro thumbnail collection (see My collection) and can be switched off there.
  • The three numbers at the top: how many items the feed holds, how many look like remaster news and how many sources are switched on. The first one is not a running total: the robot keeps the last two weeks and drops anything older, so the label says how far back the items go ("Items · 9 days") and the number levels off once two weeks are in. A number that stays the same does not mean the robot has stopped; Last sync in the banner is what tells you when it last ran.
  • Most Mentioned Games, on the Most mentioned tab: every PS2 game the feed names, the most mentioned first, with each one's mention count and share of all game mentions. An item that names several games counts as a mention of each. It recalculates whenever the feed loads or you switch a source on or off.
  • Lists that fold away: the last 24 hours shows its first ten games, the Demand Index its first ten, and Most Mentioned Games and each list of the rarest games their first 25. Press Show … more under a list to open the rest (up to 25 games for the Demand Index, every game for Most Mentioned and the rarest games), and Show fewer to fold it again; that button follows you down the list. A list stays the way you left it until you reload the page.
  • Striped rows: every second row of those lists and of the table sits on a slightly lighter shade, so your eye can follow one row across.
  • The table: it opens on the items that name a PS2 game, and the line just above it says how many those are and out of how many. Everything else that was collected is still there: choose Everything in the first menu to see it, or Remaster News Only for remaster news, or Digital-Only News for news about games released digital-only and discs being phased out (any game, not only PS2s, so you can see how much of it there is: the line above the table counts the last two weeks and the last seven days, and how many have been archived in all; the archive is updated every 6 hours, so it can trail the feed by a few stories). Every such story is also kept for good, with its link, in data/archive/digital on GitHub, a month to a file, so the count keeps growing after the feed's two weeks. If the robot's words are changed and a story no longer counts, it is taken out of the archive too. Keeping them costs nothing extra: they come from the headlines the robot already reads, so no extra request is made to Reddit or any other site. Those rows carry a blue Digital-only tag in every view. The robot spots them by their headline: "digital-only", "all-digital", "no physical edition", "skipping physical release", "ditch discs", "discless", game-key cards and the physical-media debate; a physical release being announced does not count. The search box looks at headlines, sources and every game an item names, and the second menu narrows the table to one kind of source. When an item names more than one game, its row shows the first with its prices (or the game you clicked in the list at the top) and lists the others under it ("also names …"); a game the search box found is listed before the rest. Each row reads, left to right: where and when it came from, the matched game with its prices (press Shops under the game for links to price guides and every eBay site), the headline, and a link to the original.
  • On a phone: the table becomes a list of cards, 25 to a page, and each game's price links sit on one line that you swipe sideways.

Times are shown in your own time zone. The robot works in UTC, so "Last sync" and the times in the table are converted to wherever you are. Hover over a time to see the original.

About the 4chan rows. From 4chan the robot takes one thing only: which PS2 games a thread names. Nothing a poster wrote is copied, kept or shown. A 4chan row in the table has no headline: the Game column names the game or games the thread brought up, and the board is in the source column. These rows are never flagged as remaster news. They do count in Most Mentioned Games. Three things to know:

  • The post that starts a thread is read, and so are its replies (the latest few, and the whole thread for the busiest threads that already name a game). Only PS2 game names are taken from them; no reply is copied or kept.
  • The rules for a name are stricter than for a headline, because posts are chat. The whole name has to be there, spelt the way the PS2 library spells it, and written as a name: with its number ("silent hill 2") or with capital letters ("Top Gun", not "top gun"). A name followed by another game's number does not count ("Max Payne 2" is not Max Payne), and a single everyday word such as "Black" does not count as the first word of a sentence. Some real mentions are missed that way; far fewer wrong ones get in.
  • The Thread button opens the thread on 4chan, where you will see everything posted in it, unfiltered. Threads there are deleted after a few hours or days, so an older row's button leads to a page that no longer exists. The row stays for two weeks like any other.

About the remaster flag. Clear wording — remaster, remake, reboot, director's cut — flags on its own. Everyday words like "collection", "ports" or "returns" only flag when a PS2 game was also recognised in the same headline, because "my PS2 collection" isn't news.

2. The price links

Under every game's name, in the lists and on its own page, there is a row of small shop buttons, split into PAL (Europe) and US. They are searches, not prices: clicking one opens that shop or price guide with the game already searched for. Each shop has its own colours: Price Charting is teal with a chart mark, eBay is its four-coloured name with the country beside it (UK, DE, FR, IT, ES, US), and CeX is red. The only actual figures on the dashboard are the eBay medians. The buttons sit under every game's name in the lists, and again on its own page.

  • Price Charting tracks what copies have actually sold for, which is the closest thing to a market value. There is a separate PAL and US price for most games, and they can differ a lot.
  • eBay shows what sellers are asking today, not what anything sold for. Asking prices usually sit well above real ones, and a single graded or sealed copy can make a game look far more expensive than it is.
  • CeX is one shop's own price in the UK. It's a real "what would this cost me this afternoon" figure, but it only covers games CeX still trades and it moves with their stock rather than with the market.

Because these are searches, you may land on the wrong edition or a game with a similar name — always check the result matches the game and the region before trusting a number.

3. The Demand Index

It has its own tab, Demand. Each row shows the game's rank, its cover, its score and, under a bar, the facts behind the score. Until the daily job has run the page says there are no figures yet; nothing is wrong. It asks a different question from Most Mentioned Games: not "how much is being said" but "which games are heating up right now".

Since 14 October 2026 the score blends up to five signals. A signal that cannot be measured for a game (no YouTube video named it, no eBay history yet) is left out for that game and the others are re-weighted, rather than counted as zero, so a missing figure never drags a game down. The facts behind each score are written under it.

  • Mentions rising (30%): items naming the game in the last 3 days against the 11 days before, per day. Level scores 50; tripling scores about 100; falling to a third scores about 0. A small allowance is added to both sides so one extra headline on a quiet game does not look like a surge.
  • Sources (15%): how many different sources named it, as a share of the game with the most.
  • YouTube views (20%): the views of the videos in the feed that name the game, on a log scale so one huge video does not flatten everything else. It only counts once the scraper has recorded views (YouTube's feed carries them); until then this signal is left out.
  • eBay price change (20%): the median's change over the last week, where at least 3 copies back it. Flat scores 50; +20% scores 100.
  • Wikipedia reading trend (15%): readers in the last 7 days against the 7 before.

Wikipedia as a check. A game's article is found by search and then read in full: it must be about a video game and mention PlayStation 2 (or PS2), or the game gets no Wikipedia figure. Wrong matches found by hand are fixed in wiki_overrides.json. The same reading looks for sentences saying the game's copies have become rare or sought after, that it is out of print or was made in small numbers, or that second-hand copies sell for a stated price. Rare items inside the game, "collector's edition" boxes, a development budget, sales figures and review quotes do not count (all of those wrongly flagged games on the first run). Those games wear a blue collectible tag linking to the article. Kuon is the example that prompted it. The checks are kept in data/wiki_checks.json and redone after 30 days; the wording is matched by simple rules, so a tag is a pointer to read the article, not a verdict.

It updates once a day. Please treat the weights as a judgement call, not a measurement. Wikipedia counts readers of the game on every console, so a remake can lift an old game's trend.

4. eBay asking prices

Once an hour, a second job asks eBay what used copies of each game are listed for, on eBay US and eBay UK separately, and, since 14 October 2026, on eBay Germany (DE) eBay France (FR) eBay Italy (IT) and eBay Spain (ES), once a day each. A US copy and a UK or German copy are different products, so the sites are never mixed, and the figures stay in each site's own money (euros for Germany). The € EUR button shows all of them in euros. Germany, France, Italy and Spain are searched by the words "PS2" or "PlayStation 2" in the listing title, and the sellers' own words, such as "nur Hülle" (case only) or "défectueux" (faulty) or "difettoso" (faulty) or "defectuoso" (faulty), leave a listing out; a game with nothing listed there simply shows "none listed". Poland is not included.

The figures show in two ways. In every list (the Tracker, Most mentioned, the Demand Index and the table) and on the eBay prices tab, a priced game shows only its name and three short prices: the middle asking price on eBay US, on eBay UK, and the cheapest euro site (marked from DE, from FR and so on: the lowest of the German, French, Italian and Spanish medians). The middle one is used so a single silly listing can't drag it about. Everything else sits on the game's own page, which opens when you click its name: every site's median with its change since a week before, the cheapest copy, how many copies are listed, the complete-copy figure, how long ago eBay was asked, the price chart, links to the same search on each eBay site, and the lookup buttons. In the lists the buttons are folded under a small Shops button beside each name: click it to slide them open, click again to close. Every game with a price has its own chart, which appears as soon as the first price is recorded. Those details are only drawn when you open the page, which keeps the lists quick. The eBay prices tab has a box to find a game by name (it finds Kingdom Hearts II whether you type "kingdom hearts 2" or "hearts ii"). Until the price job has run for the first time, that tab says so and no medians are shown anywhere; nothing is wrong.

The PS2 Price Index, at the top of the eBay prices tab, is one number per site that says which way PS2 prices are going, like a stock market index. It started at 100 on 6 October 2026. Each day it moves by the typical change in each game's median since the last day that game was priced (up to three days before), so a game being added or dropped never moves it, and an expensive game counts no more than a cheap one. Beside it: how it moved since the day before and over a week, each named by the day it is compared with, and a line of every day so far. A day counts once it is over (UTC), so the latest point is yesterday's. Thin figures (fewer than three copies listed) are not in it, nor is a median that more than doubled or halved, which nearly always means the search found something different; a smaller change to a game's search words can still nudge it a little. A day when too few games could be compared keeps the value it had, and its card says so. US, UK, DE, FR, IT and ES each have their own card, and one more card, all PAL sites, combines every game on the UK and the four euro sites (a game on three sites counts three times; a site joining later never moves it). The first European days appear one full day after each site's first prices. Consoles are not in it. It shows up after the first price run that writes it.

These are what sellers are asking for copies still for sale, not what anything sold for: asking prices usually sit above real ones. eBay does not let ordinary apps see sold prices, so asking prices are the best figure available. Listings that say they are incomplete ("disc only", "no manual", "no case", "loose") are left out, as are copies in "acceptable" condition. When three or more listings say they are complete ("CIB", "complete", "with manual", "boxed"), the line also shows complete £45 (3): the median of those, with how many there were, which is closer to what a complete copy costs. eBay hands over at most 200 listings a search, cheapest first, so for a game with more than that (a + after the number of copies) the figures describe the cheapest 200. Graded copies (WATA, VGA and the like) and factory-sealed copies are left out of those figures, because they sell for many times a used copy. On the Rarest page they are counted on their own instead: a line such as none listed · new from £800 (3) means no used copy is for sale but three new or sealed ones are, the cheapest £800. That figure never feeds the medians, the price index or the market cap. When a Rarest game has no used copy listed in the site's own country but some are listed from other countries (Kuon on eBay UK), the line shows median from abroad instead of "none listed", with how many copies there are; that number is kept apart from the ordinary medians and the index too.

Price charts: the small chart button beside a game on the eBay prices tab and on the Rarest lists opens that game's price history: its median on each day it was checked, as a line, with bars under it for how many copies were for sale that day. Use the buttons above it to switch between eBay US, UK, DE, FR, IT and ES (the ones with figures), to show the last week (1W, what a chart opens on), month (1M), three months (3M) or everything kept (All, up to 90 days), to draw a Line or a filled Area, and to switch to a Log scale, where the same step up or down means the same percentage move. Move the pointer over the chart (or just tap it on a phone) to read any day's figures in the line above it. On a computer, drag to scroll back in time and scroll to zoom; on a phone, one finger scrolls the page as usual and the range buttons do the zooming. The buttons are one row you can swipe sideways. Press close, press Esc or tap outside it to close it. The charts are drawn with TradingView's free Lightweight Charts; if that cannot load (no connection to its server), a plain line is shown instead. The history starts from when each game was first priced, so a new game has a short line.

The EUR button: at the top, next to My collection, there is a € EUR button (it appears only when the day's exchange rates could be read). Press it and every eBay price, market cap and price chart on the site is shown in euros instead of dollars or pounds. Each converted figure starts with ≈ because it is worked out, not an eBay price: it uses the European Central Bank's rate for the day written beside the button, and charts and the week's change use that same rate for every day, so a chart does not wobble with exchange rates. The index lines are percentages and do not change. PriceCharting's guide figures stay in US dollars. Press the button again to go back; the site remembers your choice in your browser.

  • Which games: every game the feed has ever mentioned, as long as it is in the PS2 library. Games with a one-word name that is also an ordinary word, such as Black, Cars or The Sims, are left out, because a search for the word finds every listing that happens to use it. Those rows keep their shop buttons and simply have no median. The games on the Rarest page are priced too, whether the feed has named them or not. Limited and collector's editions (a steelbook, a boxed set) are priced separately and appear only on their game's page, under Editions of this game: each shows what it contains, when it came out, its own asking prices and a link to the page that lists it. Only listings that name the edition are counted, so a plain copy never moves them. The list is made by hand and is not complete; Japanese editions are not included.
  • Search box and game pages now also know about 800 North American and European names the library lacked (such as FIFA 09 to FIFA 14), read from PCSX2's public game list on 14 October 2026 and kept in search_extra_titles.json. They are for searching only: the robot does not match them in news or price them, so their pages stay empty until the library itself has the name. A game the library spells two ways (Medal of Honor Frontline and Medal of Honor: Frontline) appears once, and words can be typed in any form: fifa 10 finds FIFA Soccer 10, and honour finds Honor.
  • The "scarce" tag means 10 or fewer copies, and it is only shown when eBay's own count for the same search agrees with ours to within two listings; where our name checks left out many listings (a game with two spellings, or a sibling with the same words) the count is too low, so no tag is shown. The eBay figure and the tag both open the very search they count: used, Buy It Now, listed in that country, in Video Games. Auctions, new copies and sellers abroad are not counted, so the general eBay UK button beside a game shows more. A tag appears only once a game has been checked since 14 October 2026 (within a day or two).
  • One site only: when just US or just UK is shown under a name, eBay had no copy the robot could count on the other site.
  • Different spellings: a listing that adds or drops a "the" is still the game (Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time counts for Prince of Persia: Sands of Time; from 14 October 2026, before which most of its listings were missed). The feed may call a game Persona 4 or Kingdom Hearts 2 where the price list says Shin Megami Tensei: Persona 4 or Kingdom Hearts II. They are treated as the same game.
  • Games with another name in Europe: some games were released in Europe under a different name, such as Fatal Frame as Project Zero, Siren as Forbidden Siren or Dark Cloud 2 as Dark Chronicle. eBay UK, Germany, France, Italy and Spain are searched under the European name, so their prices come from PAL copies. The list of these names is in ebay_watchlist.json on GitHub; tell the repository's owner about any that are missing.
  • surging: a game that is rarely mentioned was suddenly named in headlines by two or more sources within a day. It is checked on every run for the next two days. About half of these turn out to be the robot matching the wrong game, so read it as "worth a look".
  • staple: named on five or more of the last fourteen days, so one more mention is no news. quiet: not mentioned for two weeks. Staples and everything untagged are checked about every 6 hours on eBay US and UK (a game whose price has hardly moved for days, with plenty of copies listed, waits 48 hours, and its figure is not dimmed for it), quiet games once a day, eBay Germany and Italy once a day, France every two days and Spain every three (and a game with no copies listed there waits two days). rarest: on the Rarest page and not named by the feed; checked every 12 hours, and only on its own region's site (a PAL game on eBay UK, a US game on eBay US); consoles and editions once a day. underrated: on the Underrated page and not named by the feed; checked once a day on eBay US and UK, so a game has a price the day it is shown. When the feed names a Rarest or Underrated game, it is looked up again at the next run.
  • few copies: only one or two copies were for sale, so the "median" is just one or two sellers' asking prices and can be far from what the game is worth. Common on the Rarest lists.
  • A + after the number of copies means eBay had more listings than it hands over at once (200), so the figures cover the first 200.
  • A dimmed price, on a game's own page and the eBay prices page, has gone longer than usual without a check: over 8 hours, or over 30 for a quiet game. GitHub sometimes runs the jobs late. Prices beside a name in the lists are never dimmed; hover over one to see how old it is when it is overdue.
  • "in headlines" counts headlines that named the game in the last two weeks. Most Mentioned Games also counts mentions found in the text under a headline, so the two numbers can differ.
  • "none listed" means eBay was asked and no copy was counted. "search needs tuning" means eBay returned listings but none of them named the game, so there is no figure rather than a wrong one. "not checked yet" means its turn hasn't come.
  • The list on the eBay prices tab is ordered with surging games first, then by how often each is mentioned, and quiet games last.

Only numbers are kept: the current figures, and a history of how they change, in the data/prices folder on GitHub. To always track a game, give one its own search words, or stop one being priced, the repository's owner edits ebay_watchlist.json.

5. The rarest games

The Rarest tab has three lists: the rarest PAL games (Europe and other PAL countries, over a hundred), the rarest US games (over a hundred), and the rarest official Consoles. Press the coloured PAL (blue), US (red) or Consoles (teal) buttons at the top right to switch between them; the lit one is the list on screen. The PAL list is priced on eBay UK and the US list on eBay US. Most games also carry a short note under their prices (when it came out in Europe or America and in what order, whether a region never got it, or a limited run), followed by Release facts links to the public page it was read from, usually Wikipedia. A game without a note is one where no page could be read to back one up. Under every game's prices is a PriceCharting link that searches PriceCharting for that game (the PAL page for the PAL list, the US page for the US list), so a game with no eBay figure can still be checked by hand.

  • Articles (not live): under some games a line quotes what public articles said a copy was worth, with the article's name and year, for example £600 (Arcade Attack 2023). They are old, rounded and mostly repeat PriceCharting, so they are a second opinion on the guide figure and not a price you can buy at. They are kept, with their sources and dates, in rare_reference_prices.json.
  • How they are ranked: nobody counts how many copies of a game were made or still exist, so collectors go by price. Each list is ranked by guide, PriceCharting's market price for a complete copy (game, case and manual) in US dollars, as read on the date in the line under the heading. That is where cheap "shovelware" that was printed in tiny numbers, such as Sengoku Anthology or Gun Club, comes out above famous games.
  • The eBay price under each game is the median asking price for a used copy on that region's own site, checked every 12 hours: eBay UK for the PAL list, eBay US for the US list. It works like the rest of the dashboard's eBay figures (see eBay asking prices): it is what sellers ask, not what anything sold for, and rare games often have few or no copies for sale, so "none listed" is common and a single copy can make the median jump. "not priced" means the game is never looked up, usually because its name is an everyday word (Obscure, Buccaneer) and an eBay search would count other games' listings.
  • US name / PAL name: the name the same game has in the other region, where it differs.
  • Sources: the links under each game are where its place on the list comes from (PriceCharting, and articles such as Racketboy's where they mention the game). Hover over a link for the figure it gives and its date.
  • Tags: thin data means the guide value rests on one or two sales; one source means only PriceCharting gives a figure; edition is a special edition or a set sold under its own name; India and Australia mark a PAL game released only there, which hardly ever appears on eBay UK. An eBay asking price is never counted as a second source.
  • How sure the order is: the top of each list is backed by several sources. Lower down most places rest on PriceCharting alone, and below roughly $60 (PAL) or $90 (US) the order may have gaps. The lists were researched on 8 October 2026 and widened on 9 October (more sources, a few values re-read, and games the first pass missed, such as the Australian GTA Complete Collection and the shovelware World Quiz and Monster Eggs); they are not updated by themselves, the eBay prices are.

The chart above the PAL and US lists tracks the whole list, like a stock market chart. Three figures sit at the top: the list's rare index, its market cap, and the market cap without big sellers, each with how much it moved over the range shown. The buttons choose what the chart draws (Index or Market cap, and for the market cap, All games or Without big sellers), the range (1W, 1M, 3M, All), Line or Area, and a Log scale; the pointer reads off any day, as on a game's chart (see Price charts).

  • The rare index works like the PS2 Price Index, but only for the games on that list, on that list's own site (eBay UK for PAL, eBay US for US). It starts at 100 on the first day at least 80% of the list had a price (8 October 2026), and moves on the typical change of each game's median, counting medians of two or more copies (rare games seldom have three).
  • The market cap is a rough size of the whole list's market: for each game, its eBay median × the number of copies thought to exist, added up on the first day. After that it moves like a stock index: by how the prices of the games priced on both days moved, so a game being priced for the first time, or having no copy for sale for over a week, does not make it jump. A game's last median is used for up to a week when it has none that day, and a median that more than doubled or halved in a day is left out of that day's move, as in the index. PAL is in pounds, US in dollars.
  • How many copies exist is a guess. Publishers almost never said how many copies of a PS2 game they made for Europe or America, so each game's figure is an estimate in rare_games.json, with a low, a middle and a high figure and a note on how it was reached. Where a sales figure for the game itself was found it is used (for example Kuon: about 12,000 sold in North America and 8,000 in PAL countries, far fewer than 100,000); otherwise it is the typical figure for games like it: a niche Japanese game from a small publisher (about 25,000 in the US), a PAL budget release (a few thousand), an Indian release (a few thousand), a limited edition, or a mainstream hit (hundreds of thousands). The chart uses the middle figure, × 65% for the share of copies thought still to be around after twenty years.
  • Without big sellers leaves out the games thought to have sold 100,000 copies or more, such as Silent Hill 2 or GTA: Vice City Stories: they are on the lists for one rare version, but all their copies would otherwise make up most of the total.
  • Read the market cap as "about this big, and moving this way", not as a count of money: the copy figures could easily be half or double. Its moves come from changes in asking prices, weighted by each game's estimated copies (so a game thought to have many copies moves it more); its size is only as good as the estimates.

The consoles are official Sony hardware only: limited colours, special editions, Sony devices with a PS2 inside (the PSX recorders, the Bravia TV) and development units. Custom paint jobs, shells and modded consoles are never on the list. They are split by where they were sold: press US, Japan or PAL under the heading (Japan opens first, as most of them were Japanese). A console sold in several regions is in each of their lists. Each list is sorted by price, most expensive first: by the eBay median on the site that sells that region's copies (eBay UK for PAL, eBay US for the US and Japan), then by the other site's median; consoles with no copies listed come after the priced ones, by their guide value. How rare each one is shows as a coloured word beside its name (the same key sits under the list):

  • Ultra rare: 5,000 or fewer made, or a one-off, such as the five car-paint colours of 2001 (about 666 of each per region). Very rare: never sold in shops, or only through one shop or one bundle. Rare: sold in one country only, mostly Japan. Limited: a limited edition, or a larger run.
  • never sold is a development or test unit; unconfirmed rests on one weak source.
  • Under each console, one short line: its model number ("and 2 more models" when there are several; hover for all of them), where and the year it came out, how many were made when anyone has said, and the eBay median on both eBay US and eBay UK (or only the site of the one country it was sold in). These include sellers anywhere, because most of these consoles were only sold in Japan and are listed from there. A listing counts only when it names the model or its colour and is a working console; bundles with controllers or games count, but shells, skins, custom paint, mods and broken consoles never do.
  • Three are not priced: the one-off show unit, and two whose model numbers could not be confirmed. Hover over "not priced" for why.

The lists live in rare_games.json and rare_consoles.json on GitHub, with every source and the reasons for each place. To move, add or remove a game, the repository's owner edits that file; the price job picks the change up on its next run.

The Library

The Library tab shows every game the dashboard knows as a box, like a film-diary site's grid. Boxes are in A to Z order (“The” is ignored, so The Getaway is under G; titles starting with a number are last, under #). The letter buttons above the grid jump to a section. Press US exclusives or PAL exclusives to show only games whose box-art collection has an American box and no European one, or the reverse (All regions clears it). That is a guide, not a release list: a game released in both regions can look exclusive when the collection lacks one of the boxes, and a Japan-only game is in neither. On 16 October 2026 that was 435 US and 706 PAL out of 2,674 games. Press By genre to group the boxes by genre instead, or choose one genre from the menu; the box above filters by name. Genres are a rule of thumb: the Libretro database's own genre where it has one, otherwise a guess from the words in the title, and the rest sit under Other. A tick on a box means you own it. Each game has one box however many names it goes by (the American and European titles, a publisher's name in front, a typo in the library); choose PAL or US in the bar when you add it, and the picture there follows.

Press a box and a bar appears at the bottom: choose the region (gold box) and condition (rose box), and if you like type what you paid for the copy (in that region's money; it is optional, and the game is added just the same without it), then Add to My collection. Press it again to add another copy. Added one by mistake? Remove appears beside Add whenever you own the copy the bar is set to (it says Remove one with the count when you have several); it takes away one copy of that region and condition. Game page opens the game. On a phone the long list is not drawn all at once: the first screens appear, and when you near the end a soft glowing bar and Loading more games… show for a moment while the next batch is added (the A to Z letters still jump to any section, drawing what is needed). A computer draws the whole list at once. Once you have scrolled a good way down the long list, a round up-arrow button at the bottom right takes you back to the top. The games are kept in your browser only, like everything on My collection, and the cover pictures are loaded as described there.

Accounts and sharing

My collection needs an anonymous account. Open My collection (or press Add on the Library or Underrated page) and choose Create my anonymous account. The account is a random id and a random key. We never ask for an email or a name, and the service keeps only a one-way hash of the key. The page then shows you the key: press Copy my key (or Show as a QR code), keep it somewhere safe (a password manager is best) and tick I've saved my key before you carry on. Nobody can recover a lost key for you. To sign in on a phone, show the QR code on a screen you are already signed in on (the Account tab has Show my key as a QR code) and point the phone's camera at it: Spindle opens asking Sign in on this device?; press Sign in. (If the phone is already signed in, the Account tab says so; sign out there first to use another key.) The code is your key, so show it only to your own phone and close it afterwards; the phone's browser may keep the link in its history. If you already have a key, paste it under Already have a key? to sign in on another device: the account's saved list is loaded into it and replaces any games already saved on that device; nothing saved on the device is sent to the account. The Account tab has Load my list from the account to do that again later; it only adds what is missing and never removes a game. Prices you typed stay in the browser you typed them in. A new account starts empty: games saved on a device before the account existed do not come into it.

  • What is sent: for each game in My games, how many sealed, complete and loose copies you own. It goes automatically, a few seconds after a change. Your list (game, region, condition and how many) is also saved with your account, private to you, so another browser can load it. Every browser signed in with your key checks the account when the page opens and when you come back to its tab (at most every few minutes), takes in what you added or removed elsewhere and sends what is new here, so two browsers stay together by themselves; what you paid in My games, your name and your address stay on your device (the service sees your network address for a moment to limit sign-ups, and does not store it).
  • Report a purchase (a tab that appears once you have an account): game, region, condition, price, currency and the month you bought it. Sending one is up to you.
  • What is shown: a figure appears on a game's page (What collectors say they paid, and how many collectors share the game) only when five or more different people agree. A price far from the others is held back, not deleted, and counts again if most people come to agree with it. These are what people say they paid, not verified sales, and the owner counts come only from people with accounts, so they are not the number of copies that exist.
  • The Account tab has Copy my key, Show my key as a QR code, Send my counts now, Sign out on this device (only forgets the key here; the list stays on the device, and the games hide until you sign in again) and Delete my account and data (removes the account and everything you sent).
  • Reading the figures asks the service for one public file when a game page or the Account tab opens, which shows it your address.

Underrated

The Underrated tab adds one underrated PS2 game a day, in a random order, starting on 15 October 2026 with Rygar. Each day's new game is the game of the day; the page also lists the picks of the last seven days and every game revealed so far, which you can narrow by genre or to games with an eBay UK median under £15. Games not yet revealed are not shown. The list holds 100 games, and once all have been shown they come round again in a new random order. Some titles were collected from public "underrated PS2 games" lists (Den of Geek, TheGamer, Super Phillip Central; the page links them), the rest are picks from general knowledge; famous classics are left out on purpose. Each card shows the box, the genre, year and developer, a one-line reason written for this page (never a quote), live eBay prices, and, for the game of the day, an Attention bar: how often the feed named the game in the last 14 days and where that ranks among all games named (a game nobody named shows "Not named"), and, on every card, buttons to open the game's page, add it to My collection or find reviews (a Metacritic search that opens in a new tab). A game's page opened from this tab has a Back to Underrated button (from other tabs it goes back to that tab; from the search box it goes back to Highlights). The list is a hand-made file, underrated.json; no review scores are shown yet, because none have been checked against a source.

The Gacha

The Gacha tab is a game, like the capsule-toy machines in Japanese shops. It costs nothing and nothing is sold. There are four machines: Survival Horror, Action, Open World and RPG. Each is filled with 20 capsules, five common games (three capsules of each), one uncommon game (four capsules) and one super rare game (a single capsule). "Common", "uncommon" and "super rare" are relative to the machine, chosen by hand from today's eBay prices and how many copies are listed; the list is in gacha.json.

  • Playing: choose a machine and press Insert coin. The console powers on, its disc tray slides out with a disc that wears the prize's rarity colour, and the prize appears below; the tray stays out until your next coin. One lit square on the console is one capsule left. It takes one capsule at random from those still inside, so the odds shown beside the machine (how many of each rarity are left, and the chance of each next) are the real ones, and they change as the machine empties. When the last capsule is out, the machine is filled again. Under the machine, every game in it is listed with a small cover, with how many capsules of it are left and its median eBay US asking price.
  • Coins: you get three free coins a day, and they come back at your own midnight. A turn uses one. With none left, come back tomorrow.
  • Your prizes are kept under My collection, on the My gacha prizes tab next to My games: each game once with a number if you won it several times, its rarity, and what it is worth at today's asking price. They are separate from the games you own, so they are never added to that value. Clear prizes empties the list.
  • Where it is kept: coins, what is left in each machine and your prizes stay in your own browser, like My collection: no login, nothing sent to anyone, and clearing the browser's data starts again. The only thing that leaves your browser is the request for each cover picture, as in My collection.

6. My collection and installing the site

The My collection page (the gold-outlined button at the top right of the banner, next to REFRESH) is a list of the games you own. Start typing a game in the search box (it suggests names the same way as the main search: use the arrow keys and Enter, or tap a name), choose its region (PAL or US) and condition (complete, loose or sealed), say how many you have and, if you like, what you paid for each, then press Add. The page adds up what the list is worth.

The page has two tabs: My games (this list) and My gacha prizes, the games won in the Gacha, which are kept apart and never added to your collection's value.

  • Limited and collector's editions: the search box also finds editions (type the game's name, or steelbook, tin, slipcase). Choosing one ticks the region it was sold in, and it is valued at its own eBay asking price, not the plain game's; if it has not been priced yet it shows no value. The list of editions is made by hand and is not complete. An edition's cover is its own box where a picture of it is kept with the site (a dozen so far, shown on the tile and on the edition's card on the game page), otherwise the game's usual box for its region, so a steelbook without one shows the standard box; a small badge on its tile says what it comes in (steelbook, tin, slipcase and so on).
  • Portfolio chart: above your games, a chart of what the whole list is worth day by day, from the eBay asking prices the site has recorded (about 90 days at most; it needs two days of prices to draw). The top row shows the value now, the latest day's change, the change over the range you pick (1W, 1M, 3M or All) and what you paid. If you typed a price paid on any games, a yellow line marks the total paid: the value line above it means a profit, below it a loss, and Profit / loss colours it green above the yellow line and red below. If only some games have a price paid, the box says how many, and the yellow line counts only those. It shows your list as it is today, as if you had owned each game all along; the last point is the same number as the estimated value under the title. US and UK copies are added up in pounds (or euros with the EUR button) at the day's European Central Bank rate. These are asking prices, not sales. Nothing about it is sent anywhere: it is worked out in your browser.
  • Where it is kept: only in your own browser, on this device. There is no login, and your list itself is not sent to us or anyone else. Your account (see Accounts and sharing) receives only counts of how many copies of each game you own. The one thing that leaves your browser is the request for each game's cover picture, which goes to thumbnails.libretro.com and names the game (see Covers below). The side effect is that clearing the browser's data, or opening the site on another phone or computer, shows an empty list.
  • Covers: each game is shown as a small tile with its box art, like a film-watching list, with its region in the corner, a number if you have several, its condition and its value under it. The round × on a cover (always showing on a phone, on hover or focus on a computer) removes that game from the list, all of its copies; to take away only one copy, use Remove in the Library. The pictures come from the Libretro thumbnail collection (the box art the RetroArch game player shows), which has a cover for about three in four of the games in the site's library. A PAL copy gets the European box where there is one, a US copy the American one. The site only keeps a list saying which picture belongs to which game; your browser asks the collection's server for the picture itself, at its own size, only for games on screen. If there is no cover you see the game's initials instead. That server learns the name of each game shown and your internet address, as any website you load a picture from would; nothing else. Untick Show cover images to stop it entirely. If a tile has no cover, hover it: it says whether the collection has none or its list could not be read. The same cover also appears beside a game's name on its own page, and as a small picture beside each game on the Most mentioned and Rarest pages (a game with no cover simply has none there; the pictures only load as you scroll to them, and the Show cover images switch controls all of them). Box art belongs to its publishers; the site does not store the collection's pictures. A few games the collection has no picture for (eight at the moment, such as the Silent Hill Collection and the Grand Theft Auto box sets) use a small picture of their box kept with the site itself, so nothing is asked of anyone else for them. Hover or press a tile for the full details, press it to open the game's page, and press the × to remove it.
  • Backups: Save a backup file downloads your list as a small file; Load a backup file puts it back (it replaces the list on the page, so loading the same file twice changes nothing). To use the list on a second device, save the file on one and load it on the other. Keep a copy somewhere safe.
  • How a game is valued: with the median eBay asking price today for its region, PAL on eBay UK in pounds and US on eBay US in dollars, so a mixed collection shows two totals (the euro button converts them). A complete copy uses the median of complete copies when eBay has some, loose uses the overall median (listings that say they are incomplete are already left out of it), and sealed has no eBay figure, so it uses a dated figure from a public article where there is one, marked (article). A game with no figure says no price and is left out of the total, which the summary counts. If you entered what you paid, a green or red arrow shows the difference.
  • What the value means: asking prices of copies for sale, not what anything sold for and not what a buyer would pay you. Treat it as a rough guide.
  • No wallet or account: a crypto wallet could tell the site who you are, but it would still need somewhere to keep the list. For now the list lives in your browser and the backup file.
  • Installing the site as an app: on a phone, open the site in Chrome (Android) and choose Install app or Add to Home screen from the menu, or in Safari (iPhone) press Share and then Add to Home Screen. It opens full screen with its own icon. It always loads the latest numbers when you are online, and shows what it last saw when you are not. Your collection is the same one as in the browser, on that device.

7. The Sources panel

Click Sources at the top of the dashboard. There is one tab per kind of source (News sites, Reddit, Forums, 4chan, YouTube, Podcasts) and a Schedule tab.

  • Switch a source on or off with the checkbox at the start of its row. Its items disappear from the dashboard straight away. Nothing is deleted: switch it back on whenever you like.
  • Find a source with the search box, or sort the list, e.g. YouTube by subscribers or total views and podcasts by Apple ratings or chart position.
  • The numbers (subscribers, views, ratings) were measured on the date shown at the top of the panel. The Status column is live.
  • On a phone the tabs are on one line that swipes sideways, each source is a card, and the list scrolls inside its own box so the form for adding a source stays close by.
  • A changed list shows an Unsaved changes badge. Your changes only affect your screen until you save them. Discard undoes everything since the last save.

8. Add a source

Open the tab for the kind of source, paste the link into the box under the list and press Add source. The name is filled in for you where possible. Group only sorts the list, and Role says what kind of source it is (see Roles and schedule).

YouTube channel

Open the channel on YouTube and copy the address from the address bar, e.g. https://www.youtube.com/@IGN. Video links don't work. The robot looks the channel up on its next run; if it can't, the status says so (see below).

Podcast

Find the show on Apple Podcasts and paste its link, e.g. https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/retronauts/id672857593. The name and feed are filled in automatically. Spotify links can't be used because Spotify doesn't publish feeds.

News site

Paste the site's RSS feed link. Many sites have one at their address followed by /feed or /rss; otherwise look for an RSS icon. Give it a name.

Forum

Paste the RSS feed link of one board of the forum and give it a name. On many forums it is the board's address followed by index.rss. Be ready for it not to work: many big forums refuse visitors that come from GitHub's computers, which is where the robot runs. The status will say "Failing: HTTP 403" after the next run; switch the forum off again if it does.

4chan board

Type the board's short name (vr, /vr/) or paste its link. As with the other boards, only the names of the PS2 games its threads mention are kept (see About the 4chan rows).

Subreddit

Type its name (ps2, r/ps2) or paste its link. Keep the suggested group: subreddits in one group are read together, because Reddit only allows about one request a minute.

Made a typo? A source you just added has a button until you save. Sources that are already saved can only be switched off, so nothing gets lost by accident.

9. Roles and schedule

Each source has a Role (press, community, PS2-only, collector, and so on) that you can change in its row.

A PS2-only source does one thing: the robot treats anything it publishes as being about the PS2, which helps it recognise games with short or ordinary names like Ico or Black that it would otherwise ignore. The other roles are labels and change nothing in what you see.

The Schedule tab has the fetch schedule: how often each kind of source is read. Choose from 15 or 30 minutes up to 24 hours for each kind. The robot keeps running and reads a kind as soon as its time comes round. Podcasts are read every 6 hours because their feeds are large. Reddit only allows about one request a minute, so the robot asks for three subreddits at a time, newest posts first, and a Reddit round takes about ten minutes. Run scraper now, or saving your sources, restarts the robot and reads everything at once.

10. Save your changes

The list of sources is a file called feeds.json on GitHub. Saving means putting the new version there, which needs a GitHub account that can edit the repository.

  1. Press Save changes. Your changes are copied and a box with the next steps appears.
  2. Click Open feeds.json on GitHub (sign in if GitHub asks).
  3. Click inside the file, press Ctrl+A to select everything, then Ctrl+V to paste. On a Mac: ⌘+A, ⌘+V.
  4. Click the green Commit changes... button, then Commit changes again.
  5. Back on the dashboard, click I've saved it to clear the badge.

The robot starts by itself and new results appear within a few minutes.

Select everything before pasting. Pasting into the middle of the file leaves a mixture of the old and new lists, which the robot refuses to read — it keeps the previous data until the file is valid again.

If GitHub first asks you to Fork this repository, your account can't edit it directly. That still works: click Fork this repository, paste as above, click Propose changes, then Create pull request. The owner gets a request, and your change applies once they accept it. You can also press Download on the dashboard and send the file to the owner.

Commits made on github.com use your account's email settings. To keep your email private, turn on Keep my email addresses private in GitHub email settings.

11. One-click saving with a token (optional)

A token is a password that lets this page save for you. With one, Save changes saves in one click and Run scraper now works. Which kind you need depends on who owns the repository:

The repository is yours

  1. Open new fine-grained token.
  2. Name it "PS2 dashboard" and pick an expiry (e.g. 90 days).
  3. Under Repository access choose Only select repositories and pick this repository.
  4. Under Permissions set Contents to Read and write (and Actions too, for Run scraper now).
  5. Press Generate token and copy it (it starts with github_pat_).

You're a collaborator on someone else's repository

  1. GitHub doesn't allow fine-grained tokens here, so open new classic token.
  2. Name it "PS2 dashboard" and pick an expiry (e.g. 90 days).
  3. Tick only public_repo. That is enough to save, but it covers all your public repositories, so keep the expiry short. Run scraper now would need the much broader repo box; you don't need it because saving starts the scraper anyway.
  4. Press Generate token and copy it (it starts with ghp_).

Then, on the dashboard: Sources → GitHub settings, paste the token, check that the repository is filled in (it is on the website) and press Save settings. Tick Remember on this device only on your own computer: it keeps the token in this browser for the whole github.io site.

The token works like a password: don't share it. This page keeps it in your browser and only sends it to GitHub. Forget token removes it from the browser. You can cancel a token at any time in GitHub's settings: fine-grained tokens or classic tokens. Saves made with a token use your GitHub no-reply email.

13. If something looks wrong

A new source still says "Not checked yet"
The robot runs all the time, and starts afresh right after a save. Wait a few minutes and press Refresh Feed. The website itself can take a few minutes to show a new save.
A headline is matched to the wrong game
It happens: the robot matches words, so a headline about a different game with a similar name can slip through. Wrong matches drop out by themselves within two weeks as old items age out of the feed. If you see the same wrong match again and again, it's worth reporting.
The number of items has stopped going up
That is how it is meant to work. The feed holds the last two weeks: every run adds what is new and drops what has become too old, so once two weeks are in, the number hovers around the same value. To see whether the robot is still running, look at Last sync in the banner and, with the table's first menu on Everything, at the newest times in the table. The robot keeps itself going: every five and a half hours it hands over to a fresh run. If that ever fails, a backup starts one again, but GitHub can take a few hours to get round to it; Run scraper now restarts it at once.
A game has no eBay median under its name
It isn't priced. Either its name is one ordinary word (Black, Cars, The Sims), or it isn't in the PS2 library, or eBay had no copy that could be counted. In the last case the game is on the eBay prices tab, marked "none listed" or "search needs tuning"; in the first two it isn't on that tab at all.
The eBay prices tab says the price file could not be read
The page asked for the prices and got no proper answer. Press REFRESH; a passing hiccup clears itself, and prices already on screen are kept while it lasts. If the message stays for hours, tell the repository's owner: the file data/prices/latest.json may be damaged.
A game on the eBay prices tab says "search needs tuning"
eBay returned listings, but none of them named the game the way the PS2 library spells it, so no figure is shown. It usually means sellers shorten the title. The owner can fix it by adding the game to ebay_watchlist.json with the words sellers actually use.
A YouTube channel says "Failing: channel link..."
The link didn't lead to a channel. Open it to check. You can also add the channel by its ID: on the channel page open more about this channel → Share channel → Copy channel ID and paste that (it starts with UC).
"Failing: already listed as ..."
That channel is already in the list under another name. Switch the new one off.
A site, forum or podcast says "Failing: HTTP 403" or "HTTP 404"
The site blocks the robot or moved its feed. Switch it off, or add its new feed link. Forums are the usual case for 403: many sit behind a check that turns away GitHub's computers even though the same link opens fine in your browser.
The Thread button on a 4chan row opens a "404 Not Found" page
The thread has ended. 4chan deletes threads once they fall off the board, often within hours. The row itself is still right: the game was named there when the robot looked.
A 4chan board seems to mention very few games
Expected. The robot reads only the post that opens each thread, only counts names spelt exactly and written as names, and sees a busy board like /v/ only as it stands when a run happens, so threads that come and go between runs are never seen.
"feeds.json was changed on GitHub after this page loaded"
Someone else saved in the meantime. Reload the page and make your change again, so theirs isn't lost.
"Unsaved changes" doesn't go away
Finish the save steps and click I've saved it, or press Discard to go back to the saved list.

Most are in other languages (the keyword matching only understands English) or mostly play Minecraft, Roblox or Fortnite, which says little about PS2 games. They are all in the list, so switch any of them on if you want.
Does this page know any prices?
Only eBay asking prices: what sellers want today, shown under each game's name and in full on the eBay prices tab. It knows nothing about what games have sold for. The shop buttons are searches that open a shop or price guide with the game already looked up.
Why is a game high in Most Mentioned but missing from the Demand Index?
They measure different things. Most Mentioned counts what the sources here are talking about; the Demand Index looks for games whose mentions, YouTube views, eBay prices and Wikipedia reading are rising. A game can be all over the news without anyone looking it up, and the other way round.
Why do 4chan rows have no headline?
On purpose. Those boards are tracked for the game names people bring up, and for nothing else, so nothing a poster wrote is stored or shown here: no thread titles, no comments, no names. What you see is the game name. See About the 4chan rows.
Where do the subscriber and rating numbers come from?
They were measured from YouTube and Apple Podcasts when the lists were made. See the ranked lists in SOURCES.md.
Can I break something?
Hardly. The page checks the list before saving, the robot refuses a broken list and keeps the old data, and GitHub keeps every earlier version of the file.
